Which uncertain inputs drive measles outbreak risk?

A simple measles transmission model with births, two routine vaccine doses and periodic mass campaigns. Move the sliders to run it live, then see which inputs matter most across 400,000 runs.

This is a learning exercise. The model is not calibrated to any country and its results are not policy advice. Its assumptions and limits are listed at the bottom of this page.

Try the model

Between campaigns, unvaccinated newborns pile up and the effective reproduction number climbs. When it passes 1, an imported case can start a growing outbreak. Each campaign knocks it back down.

Which inputs drive the results?

Sobol' indices split the variation in an output into the share caused by each input. First-order is an input's effect on its own. Total adds its effect through interactions with other inputs. Computed in R from model runs, with 95% bootstrap intervals.

What came out

First-dose coverage and campaign reach dominate

Together they account for roughly two-thirds of the variation in both average burden and peak outbreak risk.

Birth rate matters as much as campaign spacing

High-birth-rate settings refill the pool of susceptible children faster, so the same campaign schedule protects them less.

Imports raise cases, not risk

Imported infections matter for average burden but barely at all for peak Reff. They seed outbreaks without changing how many people are susceptible.

Dose timing barely registers here, and that is the model's limit

With no age structure, vaccinating at 12 rather than 9 months has no cost, because infants are never exposed while they wait. The second dose also only reaches children who got the first. A model with age structure could reach a different conclusion.

How the model works

  • Two-week time steps, roughly one measles generation.
  • Each step, a susceptible person escapes infection with probability exp(−R0 × I / N). Imported infections are added to I.
  • Constant population with births equal to deaths.
  • A newborn is protected if dose 1 works, or if dose 1 failed and dose 2 works. Dose 1 effectiveness rises from 77% at 9 months to 92% at 12 months.
  • Every few years, a campaign vaccinates a share of the current susceptibles.
  • 10-year burn-in, then 20 recorded years.

Limitations

  • No age structure: no infant infections, no maternal immunity period, no age-targeted campaigns.
  • Deterministic, with no spatial variation. Real immunity gaps cluster by district and community.
  • Campaigns reach susceptibles independently of routine vaccination. In reality the same hard-to-reach children tend to be missed by both.
  • Input ranges are plausible but not fitted to data. Different ranges would give different indices.
